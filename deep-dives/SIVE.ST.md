## 1. Thesis, bottleneck and S-curve

Sivers combines qualified laser technology with access to additional production capacity at a time when optical-module manufacturers are competing for merchant CW laser supply. The decisive opportunity is the intersection of three things: demand shifting toward silicon-photonics pluggables and external light sources; established integrated suppliers directing more laser production into their own modules; and Sivers preparing several customer programs on a hybrid internal/foundry supply base. Serenity's September 8 supply-chain synthesis receives the strongest research weight. [R1](https://x.com/aleabitoreddit/status/2097200456090952096)

The investable bottleneck is **customer-qualified light at the required wavelength, power, reliability and production volume**. Ownership of laser IP alone does not guarantee capture, but access to new manufacturing supply materially improves the probability of conversion during a shortage. This is a qualified-supply opportunity across more than one architecture. It is not a claim that Sivers is the only company capable of manufacturing InP lasers.

The commercial sequence is pluggables first, then NPO and external light sources, followed by larger optical-I/O deployments. Jabil's initial orders are expected in H1 2027 and its production ramp in H2 2027. Six additional pluggable engagements provide breadth beyond that lead program. Glasgow is targeted to become operational in Q4 2027, while foundry supply supports earlier ramps. Optical I/O becomes a modeled production allocation in 2028. [R3](https://x.com/aleabitoreddit/status/2093988125144207710), [R5](#highest-weight-source-serenity-supplied-by-tom)

Sivers is pre-scale in revenue and entering the qualification-to-production portion of the S-curve. It has a broad set of credible routes to market; the most important remaining variables are conversion speed, customer allocation, qualified output and economic capture. The model includes the benefit of the shortage in customer probabilities and stable near-term component ASPs. It does not assume all prospective customers convert, or that shortage pricing lasts forever.

## 4. Hybrid manufacturing and capacity economics

### The controlling interpretation

The Glasgow statement is **more than 100M CW DFB lasers annually**, not 100M as a company-wide ceiling. The long-term internal-to-foundry model is **1:2**. Combining those statements implies:

**Internal >100M + external >200M = total >300M annual lasers**, provided the ratio applies to comparable output units and a common operating date. Tom expressly adopts this interpretation, consistent with Serenity's capacity analysis. The working numerical floor is **100 + 200 = 300M**, not 300M plus another foundry layer. [U4](sive_sources/Sivers_Glasgow_100M_capacity.png), [R1](https://x.com/aleabitoreddit/status/2097200456090952096), [R2](https://x.com/aleabitoreddit/status/2095453263613341938), [R5](#highest-weight-source-serenity-supplied-by-tom); Tom's clarification

The second foundry is unnamed. No identity or 100M-each allocation is assigned. Management's statement that substantial partner capacity is available now is economically meaningful, but does not by itself provide a quarterly quantity schedule.

The >100M statement describes the expanded Glasgow facility's total annual capability. Existing Glasgow output is not added again on top of that figure. The numerical model moves from an external-only capacity envelope to the combined envelope, with legacy products accommodated within the separate reserve described below.

### Laser, array and module units

The supplied Sivers/Ayar slide identifies an **eight-wavelength CW DFB laser array** with historical **$50–100 ASP per array**. For capacity illustration only, treat eight emitters as one eight-laser equivalent array:

- $50 / 8 = **$6.25** per emitter equivalent.
- $75 / 8 = **$9.375** per emitter equivalent, the midpoint.
- $100 / 8 = **$12.50** per emitter equivalent.

This is not proof that a discrete 70mW laser sells for one-eighth of an array, or that all announced capacity has the same die size, yields and backend requirements. The model makes that conversion explicit. Array assembly, testing and packaging can change realized economics. [U5](sive_sources/Sivers_Ayar_8laser_array_ASP.png)

### Equations and independent inputs

For each incremental laser cohort and quarter:

`Nominal quarter capacity = annual emitter-equivalent capacity / 4`

`Pre-conversion cohort units = nominal quarter capacity × scheduled loading × allocation share`

`Expected sold cohort units = pre-conversion cohort units × cohort conversion probability`

`Discrete laser revenue = sold emitters × assumed discrete ASP`

`Array revenue = sold emitter equivalents / 8 × array ASP`

All units in the tables are millions, so units × dollars produces USD millions directly. Quarters are not annualized twice. Probabilities are applied to revenue once; the separate financial model then derives costs, profit and valuation without a second Base-score haircut.

### R3: Competition and architecture

The merchant opening can narrow if COHR, LITE, AAOI, MTSI or Asian suppliers add qualified external supply, or if customers internalize more laser production. Multi-sourcing may accelerate Sivers' entry during scarcity while limiting its mature pricing power. Technical qualification creates switching friction but does not establish permanent exclusivity.

VCSEL-based approaches, alternative light-source architectures and increased laser sharing can contest parts of short-reach scale-up optics. SOA-based systems can add a product opportunity while changing the number and power of seed lasers. No product family is declared universally immune. The correct watch is design-specific production adoption, optical power and supplier allocation, not the mere appearance of another architecture or an executive hire.
